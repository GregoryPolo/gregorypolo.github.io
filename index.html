About me

Hello! My name is Gregory Polo, and I’m a computer engineering student at Syracuse University. I grew up around computers, and over time my curiosity shifted from what was on the screen to what was inside the machine. I wanted to know about the components, and how something so complicated can work correctly every single time I press a button.

That question is what led me to computer engineering. I’m fascinated by reliability: the idea that countless tiny parts, each doing a simple job, can add up to something you trust without thinking about it. Studying it felt natural, because I already felt at home around computers.

4-bit CPU on an Intel MAX 10 FPGA

February 2026, with a lab partner

  • VHDL
  • Quartus Prime
  • Intel MAX 10 (DE10-Lite)
  • Finite state machines
  • Analog Discovery
  • 25logic elements for the CPU
  • 22registers
  • 4clock cycles per instruction
  • 6states in the control FSM
The goal
Build a 4-bit CPU from scratch in VHDL on the Intel MAX 10 DE10-Lite FPGA that could run LDA (load accumulator) and ADDA (add to accumulator) instructions from a 16-address ROM, working with a lab partner and a provided set of components.
What I built
I wired seven components together (instruction and operand registers, program counter, ALU, accumulator, ROM, and the control FSM) plus six hex displays, then coded a 6-state FSM that completes each instruction in 4 clock cycles and drives every control signal.
The result
The CPU used only 25 logic elements and 22 registers. It ran the 8-instruction ROM program at 1 Hz, and then a second program I wrote myself (the accumulator reads 1, 3, 7, 9, A, 8, 6, 2). Both were demonstrated to the instructor. The display at the top of this page replays that second program.

Arduino sensor interfacing

March 2026, with a lab partner

  • Arduino
  • C++
  • Python (pandas, NumPy, SciPy, Matplotlib)
  • Digilent WaveForms
  • Analog Discovery
  • 3instruments agree on the 1.65 V reference
  • 142.7°release angle modeled
  • 17 sof pendulum decay compared
  • 2models: linear and nonlinear
The goal
Measure a joystick and a swinging pendulum with an Arduino and an Analog Discovery, then build theoretical models that could predict the pendulum’s motion.
What I did
I logged time-stamped voltage from the pendulum circuit, mapped each reading to an angle in Python (360° per 3.3 V, zeroed at 1.65 V), and modeled the swing from a 142.7° release with a linear state-space model and a nonlinear ODE solved with RK45, using measured damping, length, and mass.
The result
The 1.65 V reference matched across theory, the Arduino serial monitor, and the Analog Discovery. Over the 17-second run, the nonlinear model matched the measured decay almost exactly and beat the linear model, and both drifted slightly near the end.

Caffeine extraction

February 2024

  • Liquid-liquid extraction
  • Dichloromethane
  • Quantitative analysis
  • Lab safety
  • 15samples: 5 brands, 3 trials each
  • 3extractions per sample
  • 39%average gap below the label
The goal
Test whether five popular energy drinks (Red Bull, Prime, Monster, Celsius, and Bang) actually contain the caffeine printed on their nutrition labels, measured in milligrams per 20 mL sample.
What I did
I extracted caffeine from each sample with three dichloromethane liquid-liquid extractions in a separatory funnel, dried the extract with sodium sulfate, gravity-filtered it, evaporated the solvent on a hot plate, and weighed the crude caffeine, using a fume hood and protective equipment.
The result
I recovered 39% less caffeine than the labels on average, from 18% less for Monster, the most consistent drink, to 55% less for Prime. I traced much of the gap to caffeine left in the flask and filtration losses, and proposed standardizing the filter paper, mixing, and amount of drying agent.
